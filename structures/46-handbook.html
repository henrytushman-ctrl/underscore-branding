Handbook / Welcome

Welcome

This is the operating manual for Underscore and its member community, the Core. Everything you need to understand us — and to decide whether to join us — is in these pages.

1.1What Underscore is

Underscore is a community of builders, operators, and experts who give time to each other. Not advice-from-a-distance. Time: an hour on a call, an afternoon in a workshop, a week of looking over your supplier contracts because someone in Detroit has done it forty times and you haven't.

Our mark is the underscore — the character that sits beneath the line and holds the space. That's the posture. We're not the headline of anyone's story. We're what's underneath it.

1.2Who this handbook is for

Two readers. Members, who use it to remember how we work. And people considering membership, who use it to decide whether they'd want to. We wrote it once, plainly, for both.

Note: If you read nothing else, read Etiquette. It's three rules. They're the whole culture.

1.3How to read it

The sidebar is the table of contents. Start with The idea if you want the reasoning, or skip straight to Members if you want the people. The Join page is always one click away — that's deliberate.

Handbook / The idea

The idea

Most professional networks trade in introductions. We trade in hours. The difference sounds small and changes everything.

2.1The problem we started with

When you're building something, the help you need is rarely a contact. It's competence, applied to your specific mess, by someone who has no stake in flattering you. That kind of help is everywhere — locked inside busy people — and almost impossible to ask for cold.

So we built a structure where asking is normal, giving is expected, and nobody keeps score.

2.2The mechanism

  1. Every member commits hours — most give two to four a month.
  2. Members post asks: specific, scoped problems. (See Asks.)
  3. Members post offers: standing capabilities others can claim. (See Offers.)
  4. Chapters in 40 cities turn the online graph into rooms with people in them.

2.3Why there's no ledger

We deliberately do not track who gave what to whom. A ledger turns generosity into bookkeeping, and bookkeeping invites settling up. The system stays generous as long as everyone gives slightly more than they think is fair and nobody checks.

Note: Nobody tracks favors. Don't start.

2.4What this is not

  • Not a deal-flow network. Investors are welcome as members, not as audience.
  • Not a mastermind, accelerator, or paid mentorship program.
  • Not networking. If you leave a session with a problem solved and no new "connection," that's a success.

3.2What makes someone Core

Three things, and all three matter:

  • Competence. You've done real work and can show it. Titles don't count; shipped things do.
  • Capacity. You can actually give a few hours a month. If this year is impossible, apply next year — the door doesn't close.
  • Temperament. You can give help without attaching strings, and take help without performing gratitude.
Note: "Since" in the roster is membership year, not seniority. A 2025 member's hour is worth exactly what a 2021 member's hour is worth.

3.3Finding each other

Members search the directory by tag — the mono labels above. Tags describe what someone gives, not what they are. If your problem is "our injection molds keep failing," you search _tooling and find Tom, not a job title.

4.2What an "hour given" is

One member's time, applied to another member's problem, logged by the giver after the fact. Office hours, workshop sessions, reviews, site visits. We round down, we don't log social time, and we don't log anything the giver was paid for.

4.3What we don't measure

Individual totals, leaderboards, response rates, "engagement." We tried a dashboard in 2022. It made generous people competitive and competitive people insufferable. We deleted it and kept the four numbers above.

Note: 9,000 hours is the community total. Your personal number is between you and nobody.
Handbook / The Core / Chapters

Chapters

Forty cities, each with a host, a recurring room, and a calendar. Chapters are where asks become afternoons.

5.1What a chapter does

  • Workshop nights — members bring one live problem each; the room works them in 25-minute rounds.
  • Office hours — a member with deep expertise opens their calendar to the chapter for an afternoon.
  • Site visits — factories, labs, studios. Tom Aiello's Detroit floor walk runs twice a year and books out in a day.

5.2Where we are

The largest chapters: Berlin (Jonas Weber hosts), Oakland/SF (Maya Chen), Boston (Sam Osei), Seoul (David Park), Mexico City (Ana Reyes), Bangalore (Priya Nair), Detroit (Tom Aiello), Beirut (Leila Haddad). Thirty-two more across six continents, from Istanbul to Tokyo to Chicago.

5.3Starting one

Three members in a city can petition for a chapter. You need a host, a recurring space, and a first workshop night on the calendar. The community team helps with the rest. No franchise fees, no brand police — just the etiquette rules and the mark.

Note: A chapter of five that meets monthly beats a chapter of fifty that meets never. Start small and actually meet.
Handbook / Giving / Asks

Asks

An ask is a scoped request for another member's time. Writing one well is a skill, and it's the most useful skill the Core will teach you.

6.1Anatomy of a good ask

Context, the specific problem, what you've tried, what an hour of help would look like. Four parts, under 150 words. Here's a real one (shared with permission):

# ask · maya.chen · _supplychain

CONTEXT   Hardware startup, 11 people. Our enclosure
          supplier missed two ship dates and went quiet.
PROBLEM   I need to decide this week: dual-source now
          or push the launch six weeks.
TRIED     Got one backup quote (+40% unit cost).
          Read their last three emails for signal. Nothing.
THE HOUR  Walk my decision tree with me. You've fired
          a supplier before; I haven't.

6.2What a weak ask looks like

# ask · (don't do this)

"Would love to pick someone's brain about
manufacturing sometime!"

No context, no problem, no scope, no respect for the hour. Nobody can help with this, so nobody will.

6.3Rules of thumb

  • Ask for an hour, not a relationship. Relationships happen on their own.
  • One ask, one problem. Two problems is two asks.
  • Say what you've already tried. It saves the giver twenty minutes and proves the ask is real.
  • Close the loop. When it's resolved, tell the person who helped what happened.
Note: Asking is a contribution. A good ask gives the room a real problem to work — that's worth more than another polite offer.
Handbook / Giving / Offers

Offers

An offer is a standing capability other members can claim. The best ones are narrow, concrete, and slightly unglamorous.

7.1Anatomy of a good offer

# offer · sam.osei · _infra _codereview

WHAT      I'll review your deploy pipeline or your
          worst 500 lines of backend code.
FORMAT    90 minutes, screen share, you drive.
GOOD FIT  Teams of 1–10 without a senior infra person.
NOT       Architecture-from-scratch. Bring something
          that exists and hurts.
CLAIM     2 slots/month. Book via the directory.

7.2Narrow beats broad

"Happy to help with anything!" generates nothing. "I'll read your first customs declaration before you file it" (Yusuf, Istanbul) gets claimed every month. Specificity tells people exactly when to think of you.

7.3Standing offers this quarter

  • Ana Reyes — a one-hour teardown of your brand's first impression, screen-recorded.
  • Claire Dubois — 45 minutes on any contract under ten pages, before you sign it.
  • Priya Nair — pressure-test your pricing page against three buyer personas.
  • Tom Aiello — phone call from the factory floor: bring a part that keeps failing.
  • Leila Haddad — research methods clinic: is your survey actually measuring anything?
Note: Renew or retire your offer each quarter. A stale offer that nobody can actually claim is worse than none.
Handbook / Giving / Etiquette

Etiquette

Three rules. We've run on them since 2021 and they haven't needed amending.

8.1Give without strings

Help is not a pitch, a sales call, or a favor to be redeemed. If you give an hour hoping for something back, you gave a sales meeting, not an hour. Members who treat the Core as a pipeline get one conversation about it, then they're out.

8.2Take without ceremony

Accept help plainly. You don't owe a gift, a LinkedIn essay, or effusive thanks. You owe two things: show up prepared, and close the loop afterward. That's it.

Note: Nobody tracks favors. Don't start. The moment you "owe" someone, you've imported the economy we built this to escape.

8.3Keep the room honest

Frank is kind. If the plan won't work, say so in the room, not in the hallway after. We hold confidences absolutely — what's shared in a session stays there — which is exactly what makes honesty affordable.

8.4How it's enforced

Lightly, and almost never. Chapter hosts handle friction privately. In five years, eleven people have been asked to leave. The etiquette holds because members chose it, not because anyone polices it.

Handbook / History

History

Short version: a borrowed conference room, a rule against keeping score, and five years of compounding.

9.12021 — the first room

Eleven people in Oakland, convened by Maya Chen, each with one live problem and one hour to give. The format that night — one problem, one room, 25-minute rounds — is still the workshop format today. The name came from the sign-up sheet: every attendee was listed lowercase with an underscore where an affiliation would normally go.

9.22022 — the deleted dashboard

We grew to 150 members and four cities, and made our one big mistake: a contribution dashboard with per-member hour counts. Within a month it had a leaderboard culture. We deleted it, wrote the no-ledger rule, and grew faster afterward. The lesson is in The idea, section 2.3.

9.32023–2024 — chapters

Berlin was the first chapter we didn't plant — Jonas Weber just started one and told us after. That became the model: members petition, we support. Detroit, Seoul, Bangalore, Beirut, Istanbul, and twenty others followed. The Detroit tooling rescue — four members spending a week getting Tom Aiello's line running after a tooling failure — became the story we tell about what "hours given" actually means.

9.42025–today

1,200+ members, 40 cities, 9,000 hours given. The number we watch isn't growth — it's whether asks still get answered inside a week. They do.

Handbook / Join

Join the Core

Membership is an application, not a purchase. We read every one, and we reply within two weeks either way.

10.1What we look for

The three things from the Members page: competence, capacity, temperament. The form below is built around them. Plain answers beat polished ones.

10.2Application

Received.

Thanks — your application is in. A member from the nearest chapter reads it first; you'll hear from us within two weeks. In the meantime, the rest of this handbook is exactly how we work. No surprises later.

10.3What happens after

  1. A chapter member reads your application within a week.
  2. If it's a fit, you get a 30-minute call — them helping you with the ask you wrote above. The interview is a session.
  3. You attend one workshop night as a guest. Then both sides decide.
Note: Dues are $0. The commitment is hours, and we'd rather have your two real hours than your money.
