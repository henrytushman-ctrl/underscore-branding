Underscore is a community of people who give time to each other. That is the whole model. There is no membership fee, no speaker series sold by the seat, no app with a feed to refresh. There is a list of names, a ledger of hours, and a rule old enough to be unfashionable: give first, and don’t keep score too carefully.

The name is the gesture. An underscore is the lowest mark on the line — the character that joins words without asking to be read. That is the job description. Most of what makes anything work never makes the announcement: the introduction made in the right week, the architecture review that prevented a year of rewriting, the phone call at two in the morning about a container stuck in a port. We named ourselves after the part that holds things up.

We started in 20211 with eleven people in a borrowed room in Oakland, comparing notes on problems none of us could solve alone. Nobody pitched anybody. Somebody fixed somebody else’s supply contract on a napkin, and we understood we had found a shape worth keeping. Today the Core is 1,200+ members2 across 40 cities,3 and the napkin has become a practice.

Who are these people? Builders, mostly — the word stretched honestly. founded a company and now spends her Friday mornings on other people’s. reads strangers’ system designs the way other people read novels. redraws decks for ideas she believes in. answers the logistics questions no search engine can. turns hunches into evidence. has walked four founders through their first production run, tolerances and all. and host the tables in Bangalore and Berlin. None of them is paid to do this. All of them will tell you they get back more than they give, and the strange thing is the arithmetic supports them.

Here is the arithmetic. Since the beginning, members have given 9,000 hours4 to each other — office hours, introductions, code reviews, hard calls taken at bad times. Not billed. Given. And here is the number we are proudest of, the one that explains everything else: asks that went unanswered last year — zero.5 Every member who raised a hand was answered by another member. We did not engineer that with incentives. It is simply what happens when you only admit people who lead with their hands open.

Why does giving first work? Because expertise is abundant and trust is scarce, and the only reliable way to make trust is to spend time without an invoice attached. A favor done without a price creates a kind of debt no one resents. Multiply it by 1,200 people and you get something the org charts can’t see: a quiet infrastructure of obligation pointed in everyone’s favor at once. Generosity, practiced in public, compounds faster than capital. We have four years of ledger to show for it.

We should say plainly what this is not. It is not networking; nobody here is collecting you. It is not a masterclass; nobody here is above you. It is not exclusive in the way that word usually means — the bar is not your title or your round size. The bar is whether you will pick up the phone for a stranger who shares your problems.6 Plenty of impressive people fail that bar. Plenty of quiet ones clear it on the first try.

So: if you build things, if you have an hour a month you would honestly give, and if you can name something you need — we want to read your application. It is short. A human reads every one, and a human replies within a week.7 The form is at the bottom of this page, in the footnotes, where the supporting material belongs. That is the joke, and it is also the philosophy. The important things live under the line.
